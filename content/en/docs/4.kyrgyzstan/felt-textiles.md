---
title: Felt Textiles
description: An overview of felt-making, woven textiles, embroidery, and domestic decoration in Kyrgyzstan.
---

Felt and textiles in Kyrgyzstan are made for insulation, floor covering, storage, clothing, and interior decoration. Their production involves wool preparation, felting, weaving, dyeing, embroidery, and assembly, often distributed among family members and specialized makers. These practices are connected to household material culture but deserve separate attention because their techniques, patterns, and circulation have distinct histories.

## Historical and geographic context

Sheep’s wool was readily available in pastoral economies and could be made into warm, durable coverings suitable for mobile dwellings. Woven bands, imported cloth, silk, cotton, and later industrial yarns added other possibilities. Trade routes and regional markets linked Kyrgyz makers to Uzbek, Tajik, Kazakh, and other Central Asian textile traditions, resulting in shared techniques and locally preferred forms.

Twentieth-century factory production reduced the necessity of household textile making for some goods, while craft schools, cooperatives, and markets created new venues for it. Contemporary makers may produce for family use, commissions, exhibitions, or tourist sales. These purposes affect the scale, material choice, and time devoted to a piece.

## Making felt

Felt is produced by matting cleaned wool with moisture, pressure, heat, and repeated rolling. Wool is spread in layers, sometimes with colored wool arranged into a design, then dampened and rolled in a mat. The rolling process may be done by several people, with the bundle turned and worked until the fibers bind. Thickness and firmness depend on the quality of wool and the duration of work.

Large felt sheets provide insulation for yurts and can be made into floor coverings. Smaller pieces are used for slippers, hats, bags, and decorative objects. The process requires coordination and physical labor, but mechanical assistance and purchased felt are increasingly available. A hand-made item can therefore incorporate both local technique and industrial materials.

## Shyrdak and ala-kiyiz

A *shyrdak* is an appliqué felt rug made by cutting matched pieces of contrasting colored felt and inlaying them so that the positive and negative shapes form paired designs. The pieces are stitched onto a backing and bordered with bands. Its visual effect depends on symmetry, contrast, and the precision of the cut.

An *ala-kiyiz* is a patterned felt in which dyed wool is laid onto the surface before the felting process. The resulting design is softer and more blended than the sharply cut forms of a shyrdak. Both types can use curving motifs that are described through plant, horn, water, or abstract names. Names are not always interpreted identically by makers, and patterns change with fashion and instruction.

## Weaving, bands, and embroidery

Narrow woven bands secure yurt components, edge textiles, and decorate interiors. They may be made on simple looms with geometric color arrangements. Broader woven cloth and embroidery appear in wall hangings, cushion covers, bags, and clothing. The *tush kiyiz*, an embroidered or appliquéd wall textile traditionally placed behind bedding, may be made for a bride’s household goods or used as a display piece.

Embroidery uses cotton, silk, wool, or synthetic thread on felt or fabric. Stitch types, colors, and motifs differ by maker and market. Some items are assembled from purchased fabric and ribbon rather than entirely handmade, a common adaptation rather than evidence that craft has disappeared.

## Learning and circulation

Techniques are learned through relatives, neighbors, workshops, schools, and online tutorials. Older makers may teach preparation, color choice, and stitching through shared work, while younger artisans sometimes learn designs through formal courses. A finished textile can be a household necessity, a wedding gift, an inherited object, or a saleable product.

Repair and reuse are significant. Felt rugs can be patched, bedding covers renewed, and old textiles incorporated into new items. This extends their household life and preserves patterns even when original materials are no longer available.

## Variation

Regional workshops favor different palettes, border treatments, and proportions, while urban artisans may use contemporary colors and products aimed at small interiors. Yurt coverings require heavier felt than decorative rugs, and the availability of sheep’s wool affects local production. Family taste, intended use, and access to markets all shape the result.
