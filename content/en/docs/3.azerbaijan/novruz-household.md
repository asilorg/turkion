---
title: Novruz Household Practices
description: An overview of domestic preparation, food, visiting, symbols, and local variation during Novruz in Azerbaijan.
---

Novruz household practices in Azerbaijan mark the approach of the spring equinox through cleaning, food preparation, visiting, and symbolic displays. The observance is centered in homes and neighborhoods as well as in organized events. Its elements are selected differently by families: some prepare a full seasonal table and receive many visitors, while others keep a smaller meal or observe only a few familiar customs.

## Historical and geographic context

The timing of Novruz coincides with the renewal of agricultural work and the end of winter scarcity in many local environments. Household preparation historically drew on stored grain, nuts, dried fruit, eggs, and early greens, while spring cleaning made use of the change in season. The observance shares features with [Navruz](/en/docs/common/navruz) elsewhere in the region but is given locally specific names, foods, and visiting patterns.

## The four Tuesdays

In the weeks before the equinox, four Tuesdays are widely associated with water, fire, wind, and earth. Families may name them *Su çərşənbəsi*, *Od çərşənbəsi*, *Yel çərşənbəsi*, and *Torpaq çərşənbəsi*. The sequence gives a rhythm to small preparations: cleaning, buying ingredients, visiting relatives, or setting aside time for children’s activities.

The final Tuesday is often the most active. Small fires may be lit outdoors, and people jump over them while repeating short wishes for health or the removal of difficulties. This action has differing meanings across families and is not performed everywhere. Safety, space, weather, and local custom affect whether it takes place.

## Samani and household displays

Wheat is soaked and sprouted to make *samani*, a green tray display placed on the seasonal table. Its growth is watched by children and adults in the days before Novruz, and its color is commonly associated with spring. A table may also hold candles, dyed eggs, nuts, sweets, dried fruits, and fruits such as apples or pomegranates.

The arrangement is often called a *khoncha*. There is no single required number or selection of objects. Some families include mirrors, coins, or small ornaments, while others emphasize foods that can be shared with visitors. The display may remain in place through the holiday period, then be dismantled or its sprouted wheat placed outdoors.

## Baking and seasonal foods

The preparation of pastries is a major form of household work. *Paxlava* is made from thin pastry layers, ground nuts, sweetening, and a patterned top; *şəkərbura* is a crescent-shaped pastry filled with nuts and sugar; and *qoğal* is a rounded, spiced pastry. Family members can divide tasks such as rolling dough, filling, shaping, baking, and arranging the finished pieces.

Dyed eggs are set out for eating, exchanging, and children’s games. Rice dishes, meat, herbs, breads, and sweets may form part of the main holiday meal, though the menu varies by region and resources. The foods are not confined to a ceremonial display; they are consumed over several visits and shared with relatives and neighbors.

## Visiting, gifts, and neighborhood customs

Novruz is a time for calling on older relatives, neighbors, and friends. Visitors may bring pastries, fruit, flowers, or small gifts for children. Hosts offer tea, sweets, and a meal when the visit is longer. In some neighborhoods, children leave a cap or small basket at a door to receive treats, an activity known as *papaq atmaq*. Its practice is uneven and may be replaced by other games or family gatherings.

Storytelling, riddles, songs, and informal games may accompany evening visits. Public fairs and performances offer a visible version of the season, but domestic activities often continue independently of them. The scale of a celebration changes with household size, work schedules, and the distance between relatives.

## Variation

Rural households may connect Novruz preparation to gardens, livestock, and seasonal planting, while urban households often purchase ingredients and organize visits around work days. Mountain and lowland families differ in pastries, preserved foods, and local games. Religious interpretation also varies: some emphasize the seasonal and familial aspects, while others include prayer or charitable giving. Practices are revised by each generation rather than reproduced identically.
