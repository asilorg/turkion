---
title: Carpet and Textiles
description: An overview of Azerbaijani carpet making, textile techniques, motifs, use, and regional distinctions.
---

Carpet and textile practices in Azerbaijan include the preparation of wool and silk, spinning, dyeing, weaving, embroidery, and the use of woven goods in domestic and ceremonial settings. A carpet is both a floor or wall covering and an object shaped by labor, exchange, family memory, and market demand. Textile categories should be understood through their materials and uses rather than only through decorative motifs.

## Historical and geographic context

Sheep raising supplied wool for many domestic textiles, while silk cultivation and trade supported finer fabrics in particular localities. Settlements with access to grazing, dye plants, water, and market routes developed specialized production. Weaving could be carried out in homes, workshops, or cooperative settings, and finished pieces circulated as household goods, gifts, dowry items, and commodities.

## Materials and preparation

Wool is washed, sorted, carded, spun, and sometimes plied before weaving. Its texture, color, and strength affect the type of textile that can be made. Cotton may be used for warp or foundation threads, while silk allows more detailed and lustrous surfaces. Natural dye sources have included madder, indigo, walnut hulls, pomegranate rind, and local plants; synthetic dyes are also widely used.

Preparation requires coordinated tasks. Fibers are measured, wound, and arranged on a loom, and dyeing must be timed to obtain desired shades. In household production these activities can be shared among relatives, while commercial work may divide them among specialists. The materials used are influenced by price and access, so not every carpet follows an older fiber or dye practice.

## Looms and weaving techniques

Pile carpets are made by tying knots around warp threads and inserting weft between rows. The rows are packed down and the pile is cut to a controlled height. Flatwoven textiles use interlaced warp and weft without the raised surface of pile. Kilims, bags, covers, and bands may employ stripes, geometric patterns, or supplementary techniques suited to their practical purpose.

The loom’s orientation and size determine the dimensions of the finished work. A vertical loom is common for larger carpets, whereas narrower pieces can be woven on more portable equipment. Weavers follow drawn cartoons, verbal instruction, or memorized pattern sequences. Repetition does not mean mechanical uniformity: tension, knot density, and color choice produce variation from one piece to another.

## Motifs and composition

Designs can contain medallions, hooked forms, floral elements, stylized animals, borders, and small filler motifs. Terms such as *buta*, a curved paisley-like form, refer to recognizable design elements that appear in several media. Their presence does not assign one fixed meaning to every carpet. A motif can be selected for balance, family familiarity, workshop convention, or buyer preference.

Compositions are often named after localities or design groups, but those names describe broad associations rather than strict production boundaries. Border systems frame a central field, while repeated compartments or prayer-rug layouts organize other pieces. The interpretation of symbolism is frequently retrospective; technical analysis and documented provenance are needed before attaching a specific meaning to a pattern.

## Domestic, ceremonial, and commercial use

Carpets have insulated floors, divided sitting spaces, covered walls, and furnished guest rooms. Smaller textiles carry bedding, store goods, cover animals, or wrap household items. A woven object may enter a marriage trousseau, be inherited, or be displayed on a wall once it is no longer used underfoot. Repair, washing, and storage are important parts of its life.

Tourist and export markets have influenced size, palette, and motif selection, while workshops produce items intended for contemporary interiors. This commercial context exists alongside household appreciation and does not make all weaving purely decorative. The broader objects and spaces in which textiles are used are discussed in [Material Culture](/en/docs/azerbaijan/material-culture).

## Variation

Design groups associated with Quba, Shirvan, Karabakh, Ganja, Gazakh, Tabriz, and Baku show differences in composition, knotting, color, and local terminology. Individual workshops and families may combine these features. Urban purchasers may favor small rugs or wall pieces, while rural households can retain larger functional textiles. Fiber availability, market demand, and individual skill continue to shape production.
