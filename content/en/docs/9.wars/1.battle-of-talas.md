---
title: Battle of Talas (751 CE)
description: Abbasid–Karluk victory over Tang forces in Central Asia that checked Chinese westward expansion and facilitated Islamic and technological exchange among Turkic societies.
---

## Historical setting

In the mid-eighth century, the Tang dynasty projected power deep into the Tarim Basin and the western oases of Central Asia, while the newly established Abbasid Caliphate consolidated authority after the overthrow of the Umayyads. Between these imperial systems lay a mosaic of Turkic polities—among them Karluk confederations—whose allegiances could tip the regional balance. Competition over Transoxiana, trade corridors of the Silk Road, and local client rulers created the conditions for a decisive confrontation.

## Belligerents and forces

The engagement commonly dated to July 751 CE pitted a Tang expeditionary army under the Korean-born general Gao Xianzhi against Abbasid forces commanded by Ziyad ibn Salih. Contemporary and near-contemporary accounts emphasize the presence of Karluk Turkic contingents. Initially associated with the Tang coalition, Karluk horsemen reportedly shifted sides during the battle, transforming a contested encounter into a rout of Chinese arms. Exact troop numbers remain uncertain; medieval figures are often inflated, yet the qualitative picture of a multiethnic Central Asian battlefield is secure.

## Course of the battle

Fighting unfolded near the Talas River region (in the borderlands of present-day Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan). Tang forces, operating far from their logistical base, faced an Abbasid army reinforced by local allies. The Karluk defection—recorded in Chinese sources as a sudden betrayal—collapsed Tang cohesion. Surviving Tang troops withdrew eastward; Abbasid authority and Islamic political influence advanced in the western oases.

## Outcomes and significance

Militarily, Talas halted sustained Tang expansion into western Central Asia. Strategically, it accelerated the Islamization of Turkic elites and urban centers along the Syr Darya and Amu Darya basins over subsequent centuries, though conversion was neither immediate nor uniform. Historians also associate the battle with the westward transmission of papermaking knowledge, as Chinese artisans captured in the campaign reportedly introduced techniques later diffused through the Islamic world. For Turkic history, Talas marks a hinge between Chinese imperial tutelage and a Central Asian order increasingly oriented toward caliphal and later Turkic-Muslim statecraft.

## Historiographical notes

Chinese dynastic histories, Arabic chronicles, and later Persian compilations preserve divergent emphases. Modern scholarship treats the battle as pivotal yet cautions against teleological narratives that read later Turkic-Islamic empires directly out of a single engagement. Local agency—especially Karluk decision-making—remains central to any balanced account.
