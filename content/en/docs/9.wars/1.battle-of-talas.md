---
title: Battle of Talas (751 CE)
description: Clash on the Talas River in which Abbasid forces and Karluk horsemen defeated Tang general Gao Xianzhi, reshaping power in Transoxiana without ending Chinese influence overnight.
---

The spark was local, almost petty. In the early 750s the rulers of Ferghana and Shash (near modern Tashkent) quarreled over tribute, pasture, and which empire—Tang China or the new Abbasid Caliphate—would tip the balance. Ferghana appealed to the Tang. Gao Xianzhi, the Korean-born general who already held a reputation for hard campaigning in the Tarim and beyond, answered by sacking Shash. The city’s prince fled west to Abbasid authorities and asked for redress. From that grievance, not from a grand plan to “close Central Asia to China,” grew the campaign that met on the Talas River in the summer of 751.

## Why armies gathered on the Talas

By then Transoxiana was a crowded frontier. Umayyad governors had fought for the Syr Darya and Amu Darya oases since Qutayba ibn Muslim’s invasions after 704; Turgesh khagan Suluk had humiliated Arab armies at the Day of Thirst (*Yawm al-ʿAtash*) in 724 and again in 728. Suluk’s murder in 738 weakened that steppe power. In the east, a coalition of Uyghurs, Karluks, and Basmyls toppled the Second Göktürk Khaganate in 744. Karluk groups moved into former Western Turk (Onoq) lands while the Uyghur Khaganate under Kutlug Bilge consolidated farther east. In 750 the Abbasids replaced the Umayyads, and their early commanders—among them Ziyad ibn Salih—proved more willing than their predecessors to work with Turkic contingents rather than treat them only as enemies or slaves.

Tang China still claimed protectorates across the Tarim Basin and treated oasis princes as clients. Gao’s western commands expressed that claim: he had already intervened in Gilgit and other highland theaters, and Tang prestige rested partly on the idea that a general from the capital’s orbit could punish a city like Shash and return. The Talas valley mattered because it linked Ferghana, the Chu corridor, and the Syr Darya towns—horse markets and silk routes as much as parade grounds. Tibetan forces appear in some Arabic and Chinese notices as players on the wider chessboard of the 740s–750s; whether they fought at Talas itself remains contested, but their pressure on Tang’s southwestern and Tibetan Plateau flanks helps explain why Chinese logistics already felt stretched before a single Karluk changed sides.

## Five days of fighting—and a disputed turn

Medieval totals are propaganda. Later writers invent vast hosts; modern historians treat figures such as “twenty thousand Karluks” as rounded convention rather than a muster roll. What the better-attested narratives share is sequence. Abbasid troops under Ziyad ibn Salih faced Gao’s expeditionary army along the river approaches. Fighting lasted several days in heat and dust that chronicles mention only in passing. On the fourth or fifth day, according to Chinese tradition, Karluk horsemen who had marched with the Tang wheeled and struck their former allies in the flank or rear. Arabic accounts sometimes present Karluks as Abbasid partners from the outset. Both stories cannot be wholly true; both place Turkic cavalry at the hinge of the outcome.

Chinese cohesion broke. Survivors fled east through the Tian Shan passes, a retreat measured in thirst and altitude as much as in miles. Ambush in Ferghana—country that had invited Gao in the first place—finished what the river battle had started. Only a few thousand of the Tang force are said to have reached safety. Gao himself escaped; his career did not end at Talas, but Tang willingness to project power that far west did. From a Karluk perspective the turn looks less like abstract “betrayal” than opportunistic realignment once Tang pay and prospects looked worse than Abbasid ones. Pasture rights, revenge for earlier campaigns, and the simple calculus of who would still be standing in autumn all weighed more than civilizational slogans.

Exact topography remains debated. “Talas” names a river system and a zone of approach rather than a surveyed battlefield with markers; archaeological certainty is thinner than the battle’s fame. That uncertainty has not stopped later nationalist and pan-Turkic writers from claiming the site as a founding episode. Academic caution is the better habit: the political meaning of 751 is clearer than any single GPS pin.

## What the battle did not decide

Talas did not convert the Turks. Islamization along the Syr Darya and beyond remained a matter of decades and cities, marriages and trade, not a decree issued in 751. It did not erase Chinese cultural presence in the Tarim overnight, nor dissolve Tang protectorate names on maps drawn in Chang’an. It did not invent the later Karakhanid or Seljuk worlds, and it did not make Arabic the speech of the steppe.

Even the strategic withdrawal of Tang power from deep Transoxiana owes as much—or more—to the An Lushan Rebellion that erupted in 755. That civil war forced the court to pull resources home and lean on Uyghur cavalry under Bayanchur Khan. Abbasid and local Muslim rulers faced less competition from the east because China was bleeding internally. Talas hurt Tang prestige and removed an army; An Lushan removed the capacity to replace it. Historians who treat 751 as a single door slamming shut usually skip those four years.

## Karluks, paper, and later memory

After 751 Karluk consolidation continued. By the mid-760s they had broken Turgesh remnants and dominated much of western Turkestan; Oghuz groups coalesced farther west of the Aral Sea in looser confederations that later sources call the Oghuz Yabgu milieu. Arabic and Persian chronicles later cast Talas as the moment Turkic horsemen chose the caliphate over China—useful rhetoric for writers who wanted a clean hinge. Chinese dynastic histories stress distance, betrayal, and the folly of overreach. Neither genre cared much about Ferghana’s original lawsuit or the prince of Shash’s petition.

One technological story travels with the battle. Captives taken in the campaign—or in related Central Asian fighting—are traditionally credited with teaching papermaking in Samarqand and thence across the Islamic world to Baghdad, Damascus, and eventually North Africa and Europe. Whether every artisan anecdote is literal, paper manufacture in Muslim cities accelerates in the generations after mid-century Transoxianan war. Administration, letters, and book culture sit downstream of that transfer. Silk Road exchange of crafts and seeds continued under new political ownership; Talas marked when Chinese prisoners and Abbasid markets met.

For Turkic history the quieter lesson is agency. Karluk choices, not only Arab generalship or Tang logistics, decided a major field battle. That agency would recur—Uyghur scribes under the Mongols, Turkmen settlement in Anatolia, Ottoman and Timurid armies—whenever steppe and oasis politics intersected imperial projects. Reading Talas only as “Islam versus China” flattens a dispute that began between two Central Asian cities and ended with a general counting survivors in the mountains.
