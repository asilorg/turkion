title: Seljuk-Byzantine Wars (1048–1308)
description: Prolonged conflict between Seljuk Turks and Byzantium culminating at Manzikert, opening Anatolia to Turkic settlement and reshaping the eastern Mediterranean.
---

## Historical setting

From the mid-eleventh century, Oghuz Turkic groups organized under the Great Seljuk sultanate pressed into Armenia, Georgia, and the Anatolian frontiers of the Byzantine Empire. Byzantium confronted simultaneous pressures in the Balkans and Italy, while factional politics in Constantinople weakened coherent frontier defense. Seljuk expansion combined steppe cavalry mobility with the administrative ambitions of a Persianate Islamic empire.

## Major phases

Early raids (c. 1048 onward) tested Byzantine theme armies and Armenian buffer zones. The decisive phase arrived with the Battle of Manzikert (1071), where Sultan Alp Arslan defeated Emperor Romanos IV Diogenes. Captivity of the emperor and subsequent civil strife fractured Byzantine capacity to hold the Anatolian plateau. In the following decades, Turkmen settlement and the rise of the Sultanate of Rum transformed demography and political geography. Later Byzantine recovery under the Komnenoi slowed but did not reverse Turkic entrenchment; conflict persisted through the thirteenth century amid Mongol intervention and Frankish crusading pressures, conventionally framed into the early fourteenth century.

## Outcomes and significance

Manzikert and its aftermath opened Anatolia to sustained Turkic migration and Islamization—processes measured in generations rather than a single campaign season. Greek-speaking Christian communities persisted, yet political primacy shifted toward Turkic Muslim polities that foreshadowed Ottoman state formation. For Turkic history, the Seljuk-Byzantine wars mark the transition from Central Asian and Iranian arenas to a lasting Anatolian homeland.

## Historiographical notes

Byzantine chronicles emphasize catastrophe and betrayal; Persian and Arabic sources stress sultanic legitimacy and jihad rhetoric. Modern historians integrate archaeological settlement data and caution that “Turkification” was gradual, negotiated, and regionally uneven.
