title: Mongol Conquests (1206–1368)
description: Eurasian campaigns of the Mongol Empire that incorporated Turkic populations and generated Turkic-Mongol successor khanates foundational for later empires.
---

## Historical setting

Following Temüjin’s elevation as Chinggis Khan in 1206, Mongol armies conquered neighboring steppe confederations, the Jin and Xi Xia states in northern China, the Khwarazmian Empire in Central Asia and Iran, and vast territories from Eastern Europe to the Pacific. Turkic tribes were both victims and agents of this expansion: some were crushed, others enrolled as tumens, administrators, and cultural intermediaries.

## Turkic participation and successor states

Kipchak, Uyghur, Karluk, and other Turkic groups entered Mongol service as soldiers and scribes. The empire’s western uluses—notably the Golden Horde (Jochid), the Chagatai Khanate, and the Ilkhanate—became arenas of Turkic-Mongol synthesis. Over the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, Turkic languages and Islam gained prominence in several uluses even as Chinggisid legitimacy remained a political idiom. These formations incubated later polities associated with Uzbeks, Tatars, Kazakhs, and the Timurid world.

## Outcomes and significance

The conquests reordered Eurasian trade, diplomacy, and demography. For Turkic history, the Mongol century was less an interruption than a catalytic reorganization: steppe and oasis elites were integrated into a continental imperial framework whose fragments became incubators of early modern Turkic empires. Destruction of cities such as those of Khwarazm coexisted with renewed long-distance exchange under the so-called Pax Mongolica.

## Historiographical notes

Persian historians (e.g., Juvayni, Rashid al-Din), Chinese annals, and Rus’ chronicles offer complementary but partisan views. Recent scholarship stresses ecological logistics, multiethnic administration, and the contingency of Islamization within different uluses.
