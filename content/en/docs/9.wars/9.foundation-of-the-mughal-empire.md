title: Foundation of the Mughal Empire (1526)
description: Babur’s Timurid victory at Panipat in 1526—wagon laager, gunpowder, and tulughma against a larger Lodi host—opened a Turco-Mongol dynasty in South Asia that still needed Khanwa to survive.
---

Zahir al-Din Muhammad Babur should not, by the logic of Central Asian inheritance politics, have founded an empire on the Ganges plain. He was a Timurid prince who had won and lost Samarkand, clung to Kabul, and written homesick notes about Ferghana gardens he could not hold. In 1526 he accepted invitations born of Afghan Lodi factionalism, marched into Hindustan with a relatively small army—often estimated around 12,000 fighting men—and met Sultan Ibrahim Lodi near Panipat. Lodi’s host was far larger and brought war elephants. By evening on 21 April, Ibrahim was dead on the field, Delhi and Agra lay open, and a new Timurid court had a foothold in North India. The Mughal Empire’s foundation story begins there. It does not end there. Without the next year’s bloodletting at Khanwa, Panipat might have been a spectacular raid.

## A Timurid looking for a throne

Babur’s lineage ran to Timur on his father’s side and, by proud claim, to Chinggis Khan through his mother—a Turco-Mongol pedigree that mattered in steppe diplomacy even when the real estate had slipped away. Uzbek pressure under Muhammad Shaybani’s dynasty had squeezed Timurids out of much of Transoxiana. Kabul became Babur’s base: a place to tax, recruit, and watch the roads toward India. The Delhi Sultanate under the Lodi Afghans was wealthy but politically brittle. Disaffected nobles and regional commanders preferred a disruptive outsider to Ibrahim’s centralizing grip. Invitation and ambition met.

Calling Babur’s enterprise purely “Turkish” or purely “Mongol” misses how Timurid courts actually worked. Persian was the language of high culture; Turkic idioms structured military household life; Mongol prestige idioms still colored legitimacy. The dynasty later called Mughal (from *Mughal*, Mongol) would rule as a Persianate Muslim empire over a mostly non-Muslim population—an arrangement familiar in outline from earlier Delhi sultanates, yet distinct in its Timurid court style and its particular synthesis of gunpowder war.

## Panipat: wagons, matchlocks, and tulughma

Contemporary and near-contemporary numbers for Ibrahim’s army are unreliable; what matters is the imbalance and the method. Babur arranged a wagon laager—carts linked to shelter matchlock men and cannon—tactics associated with Ottoman battlefield practice that he had studied and adapted. He combined this defensive firepower with *tulughma*, the wheeling flank attacks familiar from Central Asian cavalry warfare. Elephants, terrifying in a melee, became liabilities under disciplined gunfire and when panicked. Ibrahim Lodi fought and died; his coalition did not outlast the command collapse.

Panipat did not invent gunpowder in India, nor did it prove that “Asia” was divided into progressive gunpowder empires and stagnant others—a model historians now handle cautiously. It did show that a smaller Timurid force, properly entrenched and coordinated, could break a numerically superior Indo-Afghan army built around cavalry and elephants. Delhi’s treasures financed the next season’s politics. Babur’s own memoir, the *Baburnama*, written in Chagatai Turkic, remains an extraordinary source: part campaign diary, part ethnography of flora and fauna, part restless self-portrait of a prince who measured India against the lost orchards of home.

## Khanwa and the unfinished conquest

Victory over a sultan is not the same as consent from the countryside. In 1527 at Khanwa, Babur faced Rana Sanga of Mewar and a Rajput-led coalition that threatened to snuff out the Timurid foothold. The battle was hard fought; Mughal firepower and cavalry again prevailed, and Babur styled the struggle in religious language to stiffen his men’s resolve. Even then, Afghan resistance and local rebellions continued. Babur died in 1530 with a North Indian domain that his son Humayun would temporarily lose before a mid-century restoration. The “foundation” of 1526 is thus best read as the opening of a dynastic possibility, secured by Khanwa and only consolidated across later reigns—especially under Akbar.

What Panipat did *not* decide includes the religious character of Indian society, the permanence of Timurid rule, or the later peak of Mughal territorial extent under the seventeenth century. Those outcomes hinged on administration, marriage alliances, revenue systems (*zabt*), and wars still unfought in Babur’s lifetime. Treating 1526 as destiny flatters a single April day at the expense of the messy decades around it.

## Turkic history written in an Indian key

For the Turkic world, the Mughal foundation is a reminder that Timurid lineages did not end when Samarkand slipped from their grasp. They redirected. South Asia became the stage on which a Turco-Mongol house built one of the early modern world’s richest states, patronizing Persian letters, Turkic household traditions, and eventually a court culture that drew in Indian painters, musicians, and administrators. Architectural and artistic flowering under later Mughals—Humayun’s tomb, Fatehpur Sikri, the Taj Mahal—stands at some distance from Panipat’s wagon line, yet the dynastic thread runs back to Babur’s gamble.

The army that won in 1526 was itself a composite: Central Asian begs and their retainers, Afghan allies of convenience, matchlock men, and artillerists whose craft linked Hindustan to the wider gunpowder experiments of the Ottoman and Safavid worlds. Babur’s correspondence and memoir show a commander anxious about pay, loyalty, and the unfamiliar heat as much as about glory. That human scale is easy to lose when later centuries project the Peacock Throne backward onto a single spring campaign.

Indo-Persian chronicles celebrate Timurid destiny; Rajput traditions remember resistance and negotiated honor; European travelers arrived late and misunderstood much. Scholars still argue how much continuity linked Mughal rule to the Delhi Sultanate and how much was Timurid rupture. The *Baburnama* itself resists tidy nationalist capture: its author is too specific, too observant of wells and melons, too frank about fear and boredom, to serve as a mere emblem. Read as Turkic literature as well as Indian history, it anchors 1526 in a wider geography stretching from Ferghana to Agra—and reminds readers that empire, at the moment of foundation, still smelled of dust, gunpowder, and unfinished business.

## Further reading

This article synthesizes public historical literature on Babur and the early Mughals; it is not a verbatim digest of any one encyclopedia.

- [Babur, *The Baburnama*, trans. Wheeler M. Thackston](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/176711/the-baburnama-by-translated-edited-and-annotated-by-wheeler-m-thackston/) — primary memoir of the conqueror in accessible English.
- [Stephen F. Dale, *The Garden of the Eight Paradises*](https://brill.com/display/title/8460) — study of Babur’s world and self-fashioning across Central Asia and India.
- [John F. Richards, *The Mughal Empire*](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511584060) — concise Cambridge survey of the dynasty’s arc.
- [Lisa Balabanlilar, *Imperial Identity in the Mughal Empire*](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/imperial-identity-in-the-mughal-empire-9781848857261/) — Timurid memory and dynastic ideology in India.
- [Abraham Eraly, *Emperors of the Peacock Throne*](https://penguin.co.in/book/emperors-of-the-peacock-throne/) — narrative history of the early Mughals for general readers.
