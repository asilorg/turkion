When Greek troops landed at Smyrna (İzmir) on 15 May 1919, crowds on the quays watched a wartime defeat turn into something that looked like partition made visible. The Ottoman Empire had already signed the Armistice of Mudros in October 1918; Allied warships controlled the straits; occupation detachments stood in Istanbul. The Treaty of Sèvres (1920) would soon sketch a map that left Anatolia carved among Greek, Armenian, Italian, French, and international zones, with a diminished Ottoman stub around the northwest. That map never fully governed reality. Between 1919 and 1923 a national movement centered on Ankara fought overlapping wars, abolished the sultanate that had signed away sovereignty, and forced a new international settlement at Lausanne. The Republic of Turkey proclaimed in October 1923 was the political child of that struggle. So were population exchanges, minority traumas, and memory wars that have not ended.

## Mudros, Sèvres, and the vacuum of authority

Mudros was an armistice, not a peace, but its clauses allowed Allies to occupy strategic points “in case of disorder”—language flexible enough to justify wide interference. Istanbul’s government under the sultan retained the forms of sovereignty while losing the substance. For many Muslim Turks in Anatolia, the Greek landing—cheered by some local Christians, dreaded by many Muslims—crystallized the fear that the postwar order meant dispossession at home, not only lost Arab provinces. Guerrilla bands (*kuva-yi milliye*) formed before any single leader controlled them. Mustafa Kemal Paşa, a respected Ottoman general, landed at Samsun in May 1919 on an official inspection brief and instead became the organizing center of a national resistance that would break with Istanbul’s collaborationist path.

Congresses at Erzurum and Sivas articulated a program: the integrity of territories inhabited by a Muslim Ottoman majority, opposition to mandate and annexation, and the claim that national will—not only dynastic authority—grounded sovereignty. The Grand National Assembly opened in Ankara in April 1920 after Istanbul’s parliament was shuttered under Allied pressure. Two governments claimed to speak for the Turks; only one still held the palace. That dualism mattered. The war of independence was fought against Greek armies in the west, French forces in the south, Armenian forces in the east, and politically against the sultan’s cabinet—sometimes with open civil conflict between nationalist and loyalist units.

## Sakarya, the Great Offensive, and the end of the sultanate

Early nationalist setbacks in western Anatolia gave way to a grinding defensive success. The Battle of Sakarya (August–September 1921) halted the Greek advance toward Ankara after weeks of attritional fighting along a wide front. It did not destroy the Greek army; it denied a knockout and bought diplomatic time. French and Italian positions softened; Soviet Russia supplied gold and weapons to Ankara in a marriage of convenience against the postwar Allied order. In August 1922 the Turkish Great Offensive broke Greek lines; by early September Turkish forces entered İzmir. The human cost on all sides—soldiers and civilians, during the advance and in the burning of İzmir—remains bitterly disputed in Greek and Turkish national narratives. What is not seriously disputed is the military outcome: Greek war aims in Anatolia collapsed.

In November 1922 the Ankara government abolished the Ottoman sultanate. Mehmed VI left Istanbul. Abolition severed the residual claim that the dynasty alone embodied Muslim legitimacy in the postwar settlement; the caliphate lingered briefly before its own abolition in 1924, already outside this war’s strict battlefield chronology but part of the same revolutionary sequence. Negotiations at Lausanne (1922–1923) replaced Sèvres. The treaty recognized the new Turkey’s borders in Anatolia and Thrace, ended capitulations, and arranged a compulsory population exchange between Greece and Turkey that uprooted more than a million people under international supervision—Orthodox Christians from Anatolia and Muslims from Greece, with partial exceptions such as Istanbul’s Greeks and Western Thrace’s Muslims. Lausanne was a diplomatic victory for Ankara. It was also an engineered demographic simplification whose grief still structures Aegean memory.

## What the war settled—and what it left open

The war prevented the partition of the Anatolian Turkish heartland envisioned at Sèvres and reconstituted sovereignty as a nation-state rather than a dynastic Islamic empire. For Turkic history, it is the principal twentieth-century reinvention of a major Turkic polity: from imperial-millet frameworks toward nationalist citizenship, legal secularization, and linguistic reform that would accelerate after 1923. It did not resolve every minority question. Kurdish politics, the status of remaining non-Muslim communities, and property left behind by exchangees remained unsettled in practice. Nor did victory automatically create the cultural republic of later textbooks; that required further revolutions in law, dress, education, and alphabet—reforms whose wartime roots historians now emphasize without collapsing war and reform into one event.

Competing national memories are not optional side notes. Official Turkish republican historiography long elevated unity, Gazi Mustafa Kemal’s leadership, and liberation. Greek narratives speak of the “Asia Minor Catastrophe.” Armenian histories place 1919–1923 against the genocide of 1915 and the brief eastern republic’s fate. Allied memoirs often treat the Anatolian war as an epilogue to the Great War they had already won. Critical scholarship since the late twentieth century has examined local violence, forced migration, economic motives, and the social composition of nationalist forces—without erasing the conflict’s state-forming result. Holding those lenses together is difficult; dropping any one of them produces propaganda.

## A Turkic revolution in a shattered imperial world

The movement that won at Sakarya and Lausanne spoke Ottoman Turkish, inherited Ottoman officers and tax habits, and then renamed its political horizon as Turkish nationalism. Other Turkic peoples—under Soviet power, in Iran, in China—watched from different constraints; Ankara’s success was not a template they could simply copy. Still, the war became a reference point in twentieth-century debates about Muslim sovereignty, anti-colonial struggle, and modernization from above. Its paradox remains sharp: a struggle justified as saving a Muslim homeland ended by dismantling the sultanate-caliphate that had symbolized that homeland for centuries. The republic’s founders called that rupture salvation. Their opponents called it theft of Islam’s political center. The battles of 1919–1922 decided which group would write the constitution. They did not end the argument.
