**Navruz** (also spelled Nowruz, Noruz; from Persian “new day”) marks the beginning of spring and the astronomical new year near the March equinox. Many Turkic societies celebrate it alongside Persian, Kurdish, and other communities, blending pre-Islamic seasonal rituals with local Islamic calendars and modern civic festivals.

## Origins and timing

Navruz is tied to the solar year and the renewal of nature. In contemporary states it usually falls on **21 March** (or adjacent days depending on calendar policies). Celebrations can extend over several days with street fairs, concerts, and family gatherings.

## UNESCO and international recognition

The United Nations recognizes **21 March** as the **International Day of Nowruz**. UNESCO has inscribed collaborative multinational nominations related to Navruz on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, underscoring shared preparation rites, storytelling, music, and festive meals across participating countries.

## Turkic and Central Asian observance

Across **Kazakhstan**, **Kyrgyzstan**, **Uzbekistan**, **Turkmenistan**, and **Azerbaijan**, Navruz often features:

- Sprouting grains or wheatgrass (*semeni*, *sabzi*) symbolizing abundance.
- Thorough house cleaning and charitable giving.
- Communal outdoor events with sports, music, and poetry.
- Tables or spreads featuring eggs, sweets, and seasonal dishes; exact foods vary by region.

Official frameworks differ: some governments emphasize secular cultural heritage and tourism; religious authorities and families may integrate prayers or mosque visits according to local norms.

## Azerbaijan and Turkey

**Azerbaijan** maintains Navruz as a major public cultural holiday with bonfires, sweets such as *pakhlava*, and folk performances. In **Turkey**, Nevruz has been marked by spring gatherings and regional traditions; public narratives have shifted over time among civic, Alevi-associated, and Kurdish community observances, so descriptions should stay historically precise and neutral.
