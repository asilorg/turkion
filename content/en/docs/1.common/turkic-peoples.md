Turkic peoples are diverse communities connected by languages belonging to the Turkic family. The term is a linguistic and historical umbrella: it does not describe one nation, one religion or a uniform ancestry. “Turkic” is broader than “Turkish,” which normally refers to Turkey or the Turkish language.

## Language

Turkish, Azerbaijani, Turkmen, Uzbek, Uyghur, Kazakh, Kyrgyz, Tatar, Bashkir, Sakha and Chuvash are among the family’s languages. They have distinct literary traditions and are not all mutually intelligible. Linguists compare inherited vocabulary and grammatical correspondences to reconstruct relationships; similarity caused by contact is a different question. Chuvash represents the Oghur branch, while the other living Turkic languages are conventionally grouped as Common Turkic. Classification within that larger group remains a subject of research.

## Peoples and geography

Turkic-speaking communities live from the Balkans and Anatolia through the Caucasus, the Volga–Ural region and Central Asia to southern Siberia, Xinjiang and northeastern Siberia. Migration has also established communities elsewhere. Modern citizenship, ethnicity and language use are related but different categories; residents of a country named for one people do not all belong to that people.

The encyclopedia’s country sections cover only part of this geography. Its initial [peoples directory](/en/people) includes [Chuvash](/en/docs/common/people/chuvash), [Gagauz](/en/docs/common/people/gagauz) and [Karakalpaks](/en/docs/common/people/karakalpaks), communities that should not disappear behind accounts of large states.

## Names and early evidence

The Türk name is securely associated with the sixth-century khaganate founded in 552. Earlier steppe confederations cannot automatically be assigned the language or identity of later peoples. Modu’s accession among the Xiongnu is conventionally dated to 209 BCE. Their linguistic composition remains debated: Turkic participation is a hypothesis, not grounds to call the whole confederation the first unequivocally Turkic state.

<span id="history"></span>

## Historical change

The first and second Türk khaganates, followed by Uyghur, Khazar and other polities, connected steppe societies with neighbouring empires and trading cities. These states governed populations of different languages. Their political labels cannot simply be copied onto modern ethnic maps.

The Qarakhanids helped shape Islamic Turkic literary culture in Central Asia. Seljuk expansion and later migration changed Anatolia, while Mongol conquest reorganized much of Eurasia. The Anatolian Seljuk sultanate later became dependent on Mongol power. Conquest, settlement, trade, intermarriage and language shift acted together; a battle does not date an entire population’s conversion or language change.

Ottoman, Russian, Qing and Soviet rule subsequently created different political and educational settings for Turkic-speaking communities. Twentieth-century borders, schooling, script reforms and migration reshaped language use and collective identities. The [timeline](/en/timeline) gives a selective chronology and marks interpretive uncertainty.

<span id="religion"></span>

## Culture and religion

No single set of customs belongs to every Turkic people. Household economies include farming, herding, urban crafts and industrial work. Religious lives include Islam, Christianity, Buddhism and diverse local or secular practices. A language family is not a religious community.

Compare [food traditions](/en/docs/common/turkic-cuisine), [Navruz](/en/docs/common/navruz), musical performance and craft articles through their named local examples. Shared instruments or dishes can circulate across linguistic boundaries, while objects with similar names may differ in preparation or use. Country articles remain drafts and distinguish documented examples from broader descriptions awaiting specialist review.

## Demography and historical estimates

The previous version displayed **150–200 million for 2013**. That is an inherited historical estimate, not a current count or a newly verified census total. This overview does not update it by extrapolation. Counts must specify year, territory and whether they measure ethnicity, first language, language proficiency or citizenship; those categories cannot be added indiscriminately.
