---
title: Material culture in Uzbekistan
description: Dress, domestic objects, household textiles, and spatial habits in Uzbekistan’s everyday life.
---

Material culture in Uzbekistan includes the clothing, textiles, furniture, utensils, and spatial arrangements through which households conduct everyday life. These objects are made locally, purchased in markets, inherited, repaired, or adapted to new housing. Their use varies by region, income, age, and the urban or rural setting. A description of material culture therefore concerns habits of use as much as it does the visual appearance of individual objects.

## Historical and geographic context

Oasis cities, rural settlements, and trade-linked markets have long supplied households with textiles, ceramics, woodwork, and metal goods. Local climates and housing forms also shape the use of warm bedding, courtyard shade, seasonal clothing, and flexible domestic rooms.

## Clothing and textiles

Long robes known as *chapan* have been worn in various forms by men and women, with cut, lining, and decoration differing by period and locality. Quilted robes provide warmth in cold weather, while lighter cotton garments are suitable for summer. In contemporary life, robes may be worn at weddings, cultural performances, religious occasions, or by older people, alongside shirts, dresses, suits, and globally circulated casual clothing.

Women’s dress has included tunic-like garments worn over loose trousers, often called *lozim*, with scarves, caps, or other head coverings selected according to personal and regional practice. The brightly patterned silk-cotton fabric known as *atlas* is associated with several Uzbek textile centres and appears in dresses, scarves, and ceremonial clothing. Embroidered textiles, particularly *suzani*, have been used as wall hangings, bed covers, and parts of dowry collections. Their motifs and colours vary by workshop and regional lineage.

Clothing should not be read as a direct marker of fixed identity. A person may choose a patterned *atlas* dress for a celebration and ordinary commercial fabrics for work; a family may retain an inherited robe while furnishing its home with new manufactured goods. Material choices express taste, cost, occasion, and access to markets as well as regional affiliation.

## Domestic space

Many houses are organized around a courtyard, or *hovli*, which provides light, shade, and space for cooking, washing, gardening, and receiving visitors in warmer months. Older urban neighbourhoods and villages often have houses entered from narrow streets, with the more private family spaces facing inward. Apartment living is also widespread in cities, and new suburban construction uses varied plans. The courtyard remains an important reference point but is not a universal dwelling form.

Within a home, carpets, felt coverings, mattresses, and low tables can make a floor suitable for eating or sleeping. The *dasturxon* spread for food may be laid on a table or on the floor, depending on space and household preference. Bedding is often stored in stacked textiles, sometimes called *ko'rpacha* and *ko'rpa*, and brought out for guests or overnight use. These arrangements permit rooms to change function during the day.

## Household objects and food preparation

Cooking equipment connects domestic space to foodways. Clay ovens, metal pots, wooden rolling pins, serving bowls, and tea bowls are used alongside gas stoves, electric appliances, and factory-made tableware. A large *qozon* is particularly important when cooking *palov* for a gathering. Smaller utensils may be chosen for bread, dairy foods, noodles, or preserves. The coexistence of old and new equipment is common, especially where a household prepares both daily meals and larger ceremonial food.

Water containers, trays, storage chests, and embroidered covers also have practical and decorative roles. Gifts of household goods are often assembled for a newly married couple, with textiles, bedding, cookware, and furnishings varying according to family resources. Such collections are not static repositories of tradition; they are revised as tastes, housing conditions, and consumer goods change.

## Making, repair, and exchange

Some household items are made through specialist crafts, while others are purchased from bazaars or shops. Embroiderers, weavers, woodworkers, potters, and metalworkers supply objects that can be used daily or reserved for display. Repair and reuse remain significant: garments are altered, quilts are re-covered, and metal utensils are maintained. Relatives may pass textiles or jewellery between generations, adding family associations to objects that also have market value.

## Variation

Bukhara and Samarkand are associated with particular embroidery and urban domestic traditions; the Fergana Valley has distinct textile production and dense neighbourhood settlement; Khorezm and Karakalpakstan have their own garment and housing histories. Migration and new construction increasingly connect these regions. Material culture is consequently a changing field in which regional practices meet industrial production and contemporary design.
