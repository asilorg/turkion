title: Silk and textile traditions in Uzbekistan
description: Sericulture, weaving, dyeing, patterned cloth, embroidery, and textile exchange in Uzbekistan.
---

Silk and textiles in Uzbekistan connect agricultural production, household labour, specialist workshops, and market exchange. Silk has been cultivated and woven in several Central Asian regions for centuries, while cotton, wool, and imported synthetic fibres have also shaped local dress and furnishing. The best-known patterned fabrics, including *atlas* and *adras*, are part of a wider textile field that includes plain cloth, ikat dyeing, embroidery, and quilt making.

## Historical and geographic context

Mulberry cultivation, cotton growing, and market routes linking the Fergana Valley with other Central Asian centres have supported textile work in different regions. Fibre availability, seasonal labour, and ties between households and workshops continue to organize production and exchange.

## Sericulture and fibre preparation

Sericulture begins with the raising of silkworms, usually on mulberry leaves. The work is seasonal and requires controlled conditions during the worms’ growth and cocoon formation. Cocoons are then processed so that fibres can be reeled, spun, dyed, and woven. The organization of this labour varies: some households participate in stages of production, while factories, farms, and workshops handle larger-scale processing.

Cotton has also been fundamental to textile production and everyday clothing. It can be woven alone or combined with silk to produce cloth of differing weight, sheen, and cost. Wool remains important for carpets, felt, and warm garments. The availability and price of fibres affect what makers produce, and imported yarns or industrial fabrics now circulate alongside locally made materials.

## Ikat and patterned cloth

The term *abr* describes a resist-dye process commonly known internationally as ikat. Before weaving, bundles of warp threads are tightly bound in planned sections and dyed repeatedly. When the bindings are removed, the yarn carries areas of colour that align into a pattern once placed on the loom. Because the dyed edges blur slightly in the weaving, the motifs have a characteristic softened outline.

*Atlas* is generally a silk fabric, while *adras* commonly combines silk warp with cotton weft, though usage can vary by maker and market. Patterns may include stripes, lozenges, stylized flowers, and interlocking colour fields. Their appearance depends on the sequence of dyeing, the count of threads, and the weaver’s control of tension. A named pattern may be repeated across workshops, but colour combinations and scale are frequently adjusted for a commission or current demand.

Margilan in the Fergana Valley is widely associated with silk production and ikat weaving. This association reflects the city’s workshop histories, access to trade routes, and skilled labour, but production is not confined to one place. Textile knowledge also travels through family ties, training, and commercial networks linking towns and cities.

## Weaving and workshop practice

Weaving requires preparation of the loom, arrangement of warp threads, management of weft yarn, and attention to pattern alignment. In small workshops, tasks may be divided among relatives or assistants: one person prepares yarn, another dyes, and another weaves. Larger enterprises may separate these stages more formally. Tools and power sources have changed over time, but hand-controlled decisions remain important for many patterned fabrics.

Artisans learn through observation, repetition, and correction. An apprentice may first wind yarn, assist with dye baths, or prepare loom parts before undertaking more complex pattern work. Technical knowledge includes the properties of fibres, the effect of water and heat on dyes, and the calculations needed to place a motif accurately. These skills are often described as inherited tradition, but they also depend on continuous adaptation to materials and customers.

## Embroidery and household textiles

Textile traditions extend beyond woven cloth. *Suzani* embroidery uses silk or cotton thread on a ground fabric to create floral, circular, and vine-like designs. It can be made for wall hangings, bedding, table covers, and gifts associated with marriage. Regional embroidery traditions differ in palette, composition, and stitch work. A large embroidered cloth may be made collectively over time, while smaller pieces are produced for sale.

Quilts, mattress covers, scarves, robes, and children’s clothing show the everyday use of textiles. Fabrics may be chosen for durability and cost as well as for pattern. A household can keep embroidered or silk items for celebrations while using industrial cottons for daily tasks. Textile objects therefore move between display, storage, gift exchange, and ordinary wear.

## Markets and design

Textiles are sold in bazaars, workshop shops, boutiques, and online platforms. Designers adapt traditional resist-dyed cloth to dresses, bags, interiors, and contemporary fashion, while workshops also make standardized items for visitors. These markets influence scale, colour, and price. They do not eliminate older techniques, but they change the conditions under which those techniques are taught and valued.

## Variation

Fibre mixes, dye palettes, pattern names, and workshop methods vary between regions and makers. Household use also differs: ceremonial cloth may be kept for special occasions while industrial fabrics serve daily clothing, bedding, and furnishings.
