title: Uzbek cuisine
description: Foodways in Uzbekistan, including rice cookery, bread, market foods, dairy products, and tea service.

Uzbek cuisine comprises food practices shaped by irrigated agriculture, livestock keeping, urban markets, and household cooking across Central Asia. Rice, wheat flour, vegetables, fruit, and meat are combined through techniques that vary by season, locality, and occasion. Meals commonly place shared dishes at the centre of the table, while bread and tea organize the ordinary sequence of receiving guests and eating together.

## Historical and geographic context

Uzbekistan’s oases and river valleys support grain, vegetable, fruit, and livestock production, while its cities connect surrounding farms to markets and kitchens. This setting helps explain the shared importance of rice, wheat, seasonal produce, and large-scale cooking for family and neighbourhood gatherings.

## Rice cookery and *palov*

The best-known rice dish is *palov*, also called *osh*, prepared by cooking rice with a base of fat, onions, carrots, and meat, usually lamb or beef. This base, known as *zirvak*, is seasoned and simmered before rice is added. Cooks may include chickpeas, garlic, quince, raisins, or eggs according to a household recipe and the season. Its preparation depends on control of heat and moisture in a large cauldron, the *qozon*, rather than on a fixed ingredient list.

Regional descriptions of *palov* identify differences in carrot cutting, rice texture, use of oil, and the order in which ingredients are layered. In the Fergana Valley, varieties often use yellow carrots and a relatively separate grain; Samarkand preparations may keep components more visibly layered. Such labels are useful culinary shorthand, but recipes also differ between neighbourhoods and families. *Palov* may be cooked for weekday meals, collective work, weddings, memorial meals, and other gatherings, with the scale of the *qozon* adjusted to the number of diners.

## Bread and the household oven

Round breads called *non* accompany soups, meat dishes, and tea. Many are baked by adhering shaped dough to the heated interior wall of a clay *tandir* oven. A stamped centre and patterned rim regulate expansion and create recognizable local forms. *Obi non*, a common leavened bread, is sold in neighbourhood bakeries as well as made at home; enriched or decorated versions are associated with celebrations.

Bread has practical and social uses beyond its role as an accompaniment. It can be broken and distributed before a meal, carried when visiting relatives, or sent with a traveller. These practices are not identical in every household, but they help explain why bakeries and bread stalls remain regular points of exchange in urban and rural food economies. Freshness, keeping quality, and the availability of wheat flour also influence what is baked in different seasons.

## Pastries, soups, and noodles

Market and street foods provide another setting for Uzbek cooking. *Somsa* are filled pastries baked in a *tandir* or oven; fillings include minced meat and onion, pumpkin, potato, or greens. Their forms and sizes vary from small snack portions to large pastries intended for a family table. Skewered grilled meat, often called *kabob* or *shashlik*, is prepared at tea houses, bazaars, and roadside kitchens, where cooking is visible to customers.

Soup dishes include *shurpa*, a broth with meat and vegetables, and *mastava*, which combines rice, vegetables, and meat. Hand-pulled noodles in *lag'mon* are served either in broth or with a thick vegetable-and-meat sauce. Although these dishes are frequently described as national cuisine, their techniques also circulate across neighbouring Central Asian communities. The produce used in them changes with local harvests: tomatoes, peppers, herbs, gourds, and legumes are more prominent when fresh supplies are available.

## Dairy, preserves, and sweets

Dairy foods supply sour and cooling elements in meals. *Qatiq* is cultured milk or yogurt, while strained forms such as *suzma* can be eaten with herbs, used in soups, or served beside hot dishes. Fruit is eaten fresh in summer and autumn and preserved through drying, jams, and compotes for later months. These practices reflect the seasonal concentration of grapes, apricots, melons, and other orchard produce in many regions.

Confectionery includes *halva*, dried fruit, nuts, and *navat*, crystallized sugar often offered with tea. During spring observances, households may prepare wheat-based *sumalak* over many hours in a communal pot. Sweets are therefore part of daily hospitality as well as of seasonal and family occasions; their ingredients, quantity, and presentation depend on household resources and local custom.

## Tea and meals

Tea is served throughout the day, commonly in small bowls called *piyola*. Green tea is widespread, while black tea is also common, especially with sweets or in particular urban settings. A meal may begin with tea, bread, and small plates of salad, fruit, or dairy foods before a main dish is brought out. Tea service creates time for conversation, but the precise rules of pouring, seating, and serving differ by family, generation, and setting.

## Variation

Uzbek foodways cannot be reduced to a single standard menu. City restaurants, village kitchens, bazaar stalls, and ceremonial cooks use different equipment and work at different scales. Regional crop patterns, migration, and household preferences continue to shape recipes while keeping rice dishes, bread, soups, tea, and seasonal produce central to everyday practice.
