---
title: Music and dance in Uzbekistan
description: Folk and classical performance contexts, instruments, repertories, and social settings in Uzbekistan.
---

Music and dance in Uzbekistan encompass repertories performed in domestic gatherings, neighbourhood events, theatres, weddings, and specialist circles. They include vocal genres, instrumental pieces, narrative songs, and dances associated with particular regions. Classical *maqom* occupies an important place in this field, but it is only one repertoire; everyday and festive performance also depends on musicians whose work is organized around local audiences and occasions.

## Historical and geographic context

Performance traditions developed through the exchange of musicians and repertories among oasis cities, river valleys, and neighbouring Central Asian communities. These regional connections account for shared instruments and forms while preserving distinct local styles and languages.

## Performance settings

Weddings, family celebrations, and seasonal gatherings provide regular contexts for music. Musicians may be hired to accompany a *to'y*, performing recorded and live repertories that allow guests to dance, eat, and greet one another. The programme can combine popular songs with older folk melodies, depending on the age of guests and the resources of the host family. Sound systems and professional event venues have altered the scale of many celebrations, while smaller home gatherings continue to support informal singing and percussion.

Public settings include concert halls, cultural centres, schools, and festivals. These venues often present regional music in staged formats, with costumes and choreographed ensembles. A staged performance can preserve or adapt movement and song for an audience seated apart from performers; this differs from participatory dancing at a wedding, where the dancers are relatives or guests. Both settings shape what counts as a recognizable regional style.

## Instruments and ensembles

Instrumental practice uses plucked lutes, bowed instruments, frame drums, reed instruments, and long-necked zithers. The *dutar*, usually a two-stringed lute, appears in several regional traditions and can accompany song or be played instrumentally. The *tanbur* and *rubab* have different construction and repertory associations, while the bowed *gidjak* and frame drum *doira* add melodic and rhythmic layers. Wind instruments such as the *nay* and the oboe-like *surnay* are associated with outdoor ceremonies and louder ensemble sound.

Instrument names do not always indicate a single standardized form. Makers, musicians, and conservatory training have contributed to changes in tuning, construction, and ensemble roles. In a wedding ensemble, amplification and a keyboard may be as significant as acoustic instruments; in an archival or conservatory setting, older instrumental combinations may be foregrounded. These differences reflect performance context rather than a division between authentic and inauthentic music.

## Song and oral repertory

Many songs circulate through family memory, local performers, recordings, and teaching. Lyrical songs may address love, separation, work, or seasonal experience. Narrative performance, including epic and historical song traditions, connects Uzbekistan to wider Central Asian oral practices. In Khorezm, for example, *doston* performance joins storytelling, singing, and instrumental accompaniment. The language, melody, and narrative source of a performance can vary between Uzbek, Tajik, Karakalpak, and other communities.

Professionalization has changed how repertories are transmitted. Conservatories and arts schools teach notation, ensemble coordination, and selected classical forms, while family musicians learn through observation and repeated performance. Radio, recordings, and social media supply additional channels. A performer may move among these modes, using formal training for concert work and locally learned songs at private events.

## Dance and movement

Dance styles are often identified with regional centres, including Fergana, Bukhara, Khorezm, and Samarkand. Fergana-associated dance is frequently characterized by flowing arm and hand movements; Khorezm repertory often employs more emphatic rhythm and faster footwork. Such descriptions are broad tendencies, not complete definitions. Choreographers adapt movements for stage ensembles, while wedding dancers select steps according to personal ability and the available music.

Costume contributes to stage presentation through embroidered garments, scarves, jewellery, and headwear. In social dancing, participants may wear ordinary formal clothing rather than a regional costume. The distinction matters because dance is both a performed art and an ordinary form of participation. Gender, age, and the character of the gathering affect who dances and how movement is evaluated.

## Variation

Uzbekistan’s performance traditions are shaped by multilingual cities, regional migration, and exchanges with neighbouring musical cultures. Bukhara and Samarkand have long supported Uzbek- and Tajik-language repertories, while Karakalpak and Khorezm traditions relate to the lower Amu Darya region. Contemporary musicians negotiate these inheritances through teaching, recording, and event work. Music and dance are thus best understood as practices sustained by particular settings and relationships, not as a single national repertoire.
