---
title: Maqom in Uzbekistan
description: Shashmaqom and related classical repertories as learned, performed, and transmitted musical practices.
---

*Maqom* in Uzbekistan refers to bodies of classical vocal and instrumental repertory organized through melodic modes, rhythmic cycles, poetry, and performance sequence. The term has a broad history across Central Asia and the wider Persianate musical world. In Uzbek contexts, *Shashmaqom*, literally “six maqoms,” is especially associated with Bukhara and Samarkand, while Khorezm and Fergana-Tashkent traditions have related but distinct classical repertories.

## Historical and geographic context

Maqom repertories developed in urban musical circles across Central Asia, where Persian and Turkic poetry, instrumental practice, and teacher-student lineages met. Bukhara, Samarkand, Khorezm, and the Fergana Valley remain important reference points for their differing forms.

## Shashmaqom as a suite tradition

Shashmaqom is conventionally described through six principal modal cycles: Buzruk, Rost, Navo, Dugoh, Segoh, and Iroq. Each contains instrumental introductions and vocal sections that move through established melodic and rhythmic relationships. A complete cycle is extensive and is not ordinarily performed in full at every occasion. Musicians select sections according to the venue, ensemble, audience, and time available.

Vocal pieces commonly set poetry in Persian and Uzbek, including verses by writers whose work circulates across Central Asian literary traditions. The singer must coordinate pronunciation, melodic ornament, and metre with the ensemble. Instrumental accompaniment may include *tanbur*, *dutar*, *rubab*, *gidjak*, and *doira*, though the exact instrumentation changes between performance schools and modern arrangements. Listening to maqom therefore involves attention to both the sung text and the unfolding melodic line.

## Bukhara and Samarkand contexts

The cities of Bukhara and Samarkand have long been important centres for Shashmaqom. Their multilingual cultural histories contributed to the close presence of Uzbek- and Tajik-language poetry and performance. Courtly, urban, and domestic settings have each played roles in the repertory’s transmission. Modern concert institutions sometimes present Shashmaqom as a national classical form, but its practical learning has also depended on small circles of teachers and students.

Early recording and notation helped preserve particular versions of the repertoire, while also selecting and fixing pieces that had previously circulated through oral instruction. This process does not mean that oral transmission ceased. Performers continue to learn style through imitation, repetition, and close work with a teacher, especially in matters of ornament, phrasing, and text delivery that notation alone cannot fully convey.

## Related regional repertories

Khorezm maqom is related to Shashmaqom but has its own sequence, performance history, and instrumental emphasis. It developed in the lower Amu Darya region, where musicians combined local courtly traditions with broader Central Asian forms. The Fergana-Tashkent school includes *maqom* cycles and song repertories shaped by the musical life of the valley and the capital region. Grouping all of these as one undifferentiated “Uzbek maqom” obscures their local lineages.

Regional variation also affects how repertory is taught. A performer trained in one school may know a different set of transitions, tempo conventions, or poetic texts from a performer trained elsewhere. Conservatory programmes can bring these traditions into a common curriculum, but they do not erase the importance of lineage and local style.

## Training and performance today

Maqom is taught through specialist schools, conservatories, cultural institutions, and private study. Students learn instrumental technique, vocal control, modal structure, and repertory; advanced training requires sustained memorization. Ensembles may perform with amplified sound and staged choreography, while smaller groups preserve more intimate formats. Recordings and online video make selected performances widely available, although they cannot replace the interaction between teacher, accompanist, and vocalist.

Contemporary performance takes place in concert halls, radio studios, festivals, and educational demonstrations. It can also appear at weddings or family events, where a shorter classical song may be integrated into a broader programme. These different uses show that maqom is not confined to a museum-like setting. Its authority as a classical genre is reproduced through practice, selection, and teaching.

## Variation

Maqom carries cultural meaning through its combination of poetry, disciplined listening, and skilled ensemble work. Yet its position is not identical for every listener. Some encounter it through family recordings, others through formal music education, and others primarily at public concerts. Studying maqom as a practice makes room for these different relationships and for the regional distinctions that continue to define its repertories.
