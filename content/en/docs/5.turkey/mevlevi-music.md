---
title: Mevlevi music in Turkey
description: The musical and ceremonial structure of Mevlevi devotional performance in Turkey.
---

Mevlevi music is a devotional performance tradition associated with the Mevlevi Sufi order and the ceremony known as *mukabele* or *sema*. It joins composed vocal and instrumental music, poetry, movement, and ritual sequence. Public presentations may borrow its visible elements, but the ceremonial form has specific religious and institutional histories.

## Historical and geographic context

The tradition developed around Mevlevi lodges in Anatolian cities, especially Konya, and through networks of musicians, poets, calligraphers, and religious teachers. Its repertory draws on the Ottoman art-music system of melodic modes (*makam*) and rhythmic cycles (*usul*). Lodge closures, changing forms of patronage, and conservatory teaching altered transmission while recordings and tourism expanded public familiarity.

## The sema ceremony

The *sema* is structured rather than improvised dancing. Participants called *semazen* wear symbolic garments and turn in a prescribed sequence under the guidance of a *postnişin* or ceremonial leader. The ritual includes Qur’anic recitation, praise, instrumental preludes, and vocal sections. Its order assigns roles to musicians, reciters, and participants.

Turning is frequently reduced to a visual emblem in popular representation. Within ceremonial practice, however, movement is inseparable from prayer, listening, discipline, and the sequence of musical sections. Not every performance labeled “whirling dervish” follows the full ritual format.

## Musical forms and instruments

A *ney* reed flute often opens the ceremony with an improvisatory *taksim*, followed by the *naat*, a vocal praise poem. The *ayin* is the extended composed work that accompanies the turning sections. Ensembles may include kudüm kettledrums, tanbur lute, kanun zither, bowed instruments, and voices, although instrumentation has varied historically.

Musicians learn the mode, rhythm, poetry, and performance etiquette needed to sustain the form. Notation preserves portions of the repertory, but oral instruction remains important for phrasing, tuning, and ceremonial timing. The music belongs to a broader Ottoman classical tradition while retaining a ritual function.

## Poetry and listening

Poetry associated with Jalal al-Din Rumi and later Mevlevi writers supplies a central textual world. Ottoman Turkish, Persian, Arabic, and Qur’anic Arabic can appear in repertoire, creating interpretive demands for contemporary performers and audiences. Listening is understood by practitioners as an attentive, ethical act, not simply musical appreciation.

Public concerts can introduce audiences to the repertory, but concert conventions alter duration, audience behavior, and religious setting. Scholars distinguish between historical lodge practice, contemporary community ceremony, conservatory repertory, and commercial spectacle.

## Institutions and transmission

Training occurs through religious communities, family connections, conservatories, archives, ensembles, and recordings. Debates over authenticity concern authorization, musical competence, ritual intention, and the relation between public representation and devotion. They cannot be resolved by costume or repertoire alone. Access to teachers, archival scores, and performance opportunities also shapes which compositions remain active in any ensemble.

## Variation

Audience knowledge can range from committed practitioners familiar with the sequence to visitors encountering its musical language for the first time, often through a guided public presentation.

Ceremonies and performances differ in venue, available musicians, audience, and institutional affiliation. Some emphasize ritual continuity, others education or concert presentation. Regional and diasporic settings add further adaptations, but all require attention to whether a complete *mukabele* or an excerpted stage form is being presented. Repertory choice may also reflect ensemble size and the training of singers and instrumentalists. A shortened program can preserve particular musical features while changing the ceremony’s temporal and devotional frame. Careful description should identify the actual performance rather than assume that every turning presentation is identical.
