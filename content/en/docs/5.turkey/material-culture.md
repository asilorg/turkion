---
title: Material culture in Turkey
description: Homes, textiles, vessels, tools, and domestic objects in Turkey.
---

Material culture in Turkey includes the objects through which households cook, furnish rooms, dress bodies, store food, and mark ceremonies. Things gain meaning through use, repair, inheritance, display, and exchange rather than through age alone. Museum collections and tourist markets preserve only part of the everyday material record.

## Historical and geographic context

Anatolian environments have supplied wool, cotton, silk, wood, clay, metal, stone, and reeds for domestic production. Trade and craft centers circulated ceramics, textiles, metalwork, and furnishings across regions. Industrial manufacture and imported goods now coexist with handmade objects, and many household items combine factory-made materials with local repair practices.

## House and furnishing

Domestic layouts respond to climate, family size, and building tradition. Floor seating, cushions, low tables, carpets, and bedding stored by day remain important in some homes, alongside sofas, dining tables, and modular furniture. The *yüklük*, a built-in storage cupboard found in many older houses, accommodated bedding and textiles; its form has been adapted or replaced in newer housing.

Courtyards, balconies, kitchens, and guest rooms distribute different forms of work and sociability. Visitors may be received in a formal sitting room, while everyday family life takes place elsewhere. For bathing architecture and its social uses, see [Hammam in Turkey](/en/docs/turkey/hammam).

## Textiles and dress

Rugs, kilims, embroidered covers, towels, and dowry textiles make domestic labor visible. *Kilim* usually refers to a flat-woven textile, though local vocabulary is more specific. Motifs and colors are often marketed as ancient symbols, but their meanings are variable and cannot be assumed from design alone.

Clothing combines ready-made fashion with garments used in weddings, regional performances, and religious or family occasions. Scarves, head coverings, jewelry, belts, and layered garments have differing practical and social meanings. Handwork is transmitted through relatives, workshops, courses, and commercial production.

## Cooking and storage objects

Pots, trays, copper pans, wooden boards, rolling pins, ceramic jars, and glass containers support food preparation and preservation. Copperware requires tinning and care; it may remain in active use, be displayed as inherited property, or be replaced by steel and nonstick cookware. Bread ovens and communal mills survive in some places but are not universal.

Storage objects show the seasonal organization of household economy. Jars hold pickles and sauces, cloth bags hold dried foods, and metal tins store butter, tea, or sweets. Their contents and forms differ with available crops and market access.

## Craft, repair, and circulation

Craft workers make and mend shoes, tools, furniture, jewelry, textiles, and metal goods in workshops and markets. Repair extends an object’s life and can preserve familiar forms even when original materials are unavailable. Gifts, dowries, inherited objects, and market purchases circulate between households and connect material value with kinship obligations.

## Variation

Households also select objects according to storage space, cleaning requirements, and the changing division of domestic work among family members, including seasonal visitors and intergenerational households.

Coastal humidity, mountain pastoralism, urban apartment living, and rural agricultural work produce different domestic material needs. Class and migration affect access to handmade and industrial goods, while regional craft labels may conceal shared techniques. Material culture is therefore best studied through particular objects in particular households and exchanges. An embroidered cloth stored for a daughter’s marriage, a copper pan used every week, and a rug displayed only for guests have different biographies despite being described by the same craft vocabulary. Their value changes as they move between use, storage, inheritance, and sale.
