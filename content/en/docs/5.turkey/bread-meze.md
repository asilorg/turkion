---
title: Bread and meze in Turkey
description: Regional breads, small dishes, and shared eating practices in Turkey.
---

Bread and *meze* occupy different but connected positions in foodways in Turkey. Bread is a daily staple, a utensil for eating, and a marker of household provision; meze are small dishes served with drinks or before and alongside larger meals. Both categories contain numerous local forms and cannot be represented by a single standardized menu.

## Historical and geographic context

Wheat agriculture, milling, pastoral mobility, and urban bakery trades have produced varied bread techniques across Anatolia. Coastal trade and urban dining cultures encouraged repertoires of small cold and hot dishes, while home kitchens adapted them to seasonal ingredients. Restaurants now circulate regional breads and meze broadly, but their preparation remains linked to local grains, ovens, and markets.

## Daily breads and ovens

*Ekmek* is the general term for bread and may refer to leavened loaves from commercial bakeries, home-baked loaves, or regional products. *Pide* can describe several flat or boat-shaped breads, while *lavaş* and *yufka* are thin sheets used for wrapping, baking, or storing. Usage and recipe differ by region.

Village ovens, neighborhood bakeries, and household griddles divide labor differently. Thin breads may be made collectively by women for later drying and storage; fresh loaves are often bought daily in towns and cities. Bread is torn and shared at meals, used to lift food, and treated with respect when surplus is disposed of.

## Grains, toppings, and festive bread

Maize breads are important in parts of the Black Sea region, and sesame-covered *simit* is a common street and breakfast food in many cities. *Bazlama*, *gözleme*, and filled breads are associated with particular local and household practices but are also sold commercially. Ramadan brings distinctive bakery rhythms, including the production of soft, patterned *ramazan pidesi*.

Celebratory breads may carry decoration, fillings, or ritual associations. Their timing can relate to weddings, religious holidays, harvest, or communal work. Industrial flour and ovens have not eliminated these forms, though they alter labor and availability.

## Meze and the shared table

Meze can include yogurt dishes, olive-oil vegetables, bean salads, cheese, fish, eggplant preparations, stuffed leaves, pickles, and fried items. A dish’s classification depends on portion, setting, and service; a home food may become a restaurant meze when served in small amounts. Bread connects the dishes physically and socially, allowing diners to share from central plates.

Coastal and urban dining venues often present an array of cold dishes before hot food. At home, a similar spread may be assembled from leftovers, seasonal produce, and preserved foods. The term should not imply that all households eat elaborate restaurant-style assortments.

## Regional ingredients and preparation

The Aegean emphasizes wild greens, olive oil, herbs, and seafood; southeastern repertoires may use pepper pastes, bulgur, nuts, and grilled vegetables. Black Sea tables include maize, greens, fish, and dairy, while central and eastern areas favor grain, yogurt, and meat-based preparations. These patterns overlap substantially through migration and commerce.

Preparation often relies on chopping, salting, draining yogurt, roasting peppers or eggplant, and preserving vegetables. Small dishes can be made in advance, making them useful for visits and gatherings. Their apparent simplicity frequently depends on accumulated household knowledge.

## Variation

Bread type changes with grain, fuel, altitude, and access to bakeries; meze changes with season, local crops, religious practice, and occasion. Restaurant menus select and rename dishes for visitors, whereas domestic usage may be more fluid. Regional names should thus be treated as guides to practice rather than fixed culinary borders.
