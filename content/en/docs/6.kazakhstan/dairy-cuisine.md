---
title: Dairy Cuisine in Kazakhstan
description: An overview of fermented, dried, and preserved milk foods in Kazakh pastoral foodways and contemporary household cooking.
---

Dairy cuisine in Kazakhstan includes fresh milk, fermented drinks, curds, butter, and dried foods prepared from the milk of mares, cows, sheep, goats, and camels. These foods address seasonality and preservation as well as taste. They form a specialized field of food practice distinct from the broader organization of meat, bread, broth, and tea meals.

## Historical and geographic context

Herding households needed ways to use milk during periods of high production and retain nourishment when fresh supplies were unavailable. Fermentation, straining, salting, drying, and storing in vessels made this possible. Dairy techniques moved between families and regions with differing animals, climates, and access to market products, so recipes developed in multiple local forms.

## Fermented drinks

*Qymyz* is a fermented drink made from mare’s milk. Its preparation involves adding a starter to milk in a skin or wooden container and agitating it as fermentation proceeds. Depending on temperature, time, and starter, qymyz can range from mild and lightly sour to more strongly fermented. It is often consumed in warmer seasons and during social visits.

*Shubat*, made from camel’s milk, is another fermented drink with a thicker texture and a different flavor profile. It is especially associated with areas where camels are kept. Cow’s milk may be fermented into *ayran*, a sour drink that can be diluted or served with meals. These categories overlap with related dairy practices across Central Asia but retain local methods and names.

## Curds, cheese, and dried foods

Heating and straining sour milk produces *qurt*, a dried curd food formed into small pieces. Salted or unsalted, soft or very hard, qurt can be stored for extended periods and eaten as a snack, dissolved in liquid, or used to flavor broth. Its durability is important historically, but it also gives it a distinctive place in contemporary markets and tea spreads.

Other products include *irimshik*, a fresh or dried curd, and various forms of butter and clotted cream. Recipes depend on whether the milk is skimmed, boiled, soured, or mixed with another dairy product. Household vocabulary may distinguish products more finely than broad English terms such as cheese or yogurt.

## Vessels, labor, and household knowledge

Dairy processing requires repeated attention: milking, heating, cooling, stirring, straining, and protecting food from contamination. Traditionally, skin bags, wooden churns, cloths, and ceramic or metal vessels supported these tasks. Their material can affect flavor and fermentation, while their cleaning is part of the work.

Knowledge is often passed through observation. A learner judges thickness, sourness, or readiness by sight, smell, and touch rather than a written recipe alone. Processing can be coordinated with other household labor, especially during periods when animals produce more milk.

## Consumption and market circulation

Dairy foods appear at breakfast, tea, travel, and festive meals. Fresh cream, butter, and curds may accompany bread, while qymyz and shubat can be offered to guests. Refrigeration and commercial production have increased year-round availability for some products, yet seasonal associations remain meaningful.

Markets package qurt, fermented drinks, and curds in standardized forms. Commercial versions make foods accessible to urban consumers but may differ in salt, texture, or fermentation from household batches. Some families continue to prefer a known producer or their own preparation for particular foods.

## Variation

Milk source, climate, and local herd composition produce substantial regional variation. Camel-milk foods are not equally common everywhere, and recipes for qurt and fermented drinks differ among households. Meat-centered meals and bread foods are addressed in the broader cuisine article.
