title: Music and dance in Türkiye
description: Regional instruments, repertoires, performance settings, and dances in Türkiye.
---

Music and dance in Türkiye comprise locally transmitted repertoires, professional performance, religious expression, and staged folklore. They are learned in homes, weddings, village gatherings, schools, and media environments. Labels such as “folk” describe broad collecting and performance categories, but particular songs and movements remain tied to region, language, occasion, and community.

## Historical and geographic context

Anatolian performance traditions reflect circulation across the Black Sea, Balkans, Caucasus, eastern Mediterranean, and Mesopotamian cultural zones. Ottoman urban musical practice and rural repertoires developed in interaction but through different institutions. Twentieth-century recording, radio, conservatories, and festivals standardized some forms while making regional styles available beyond their original settings.

## Instruments and vocal practice

The long-necked lute called *bağlama* or *saz* is central to many Anatolian song traditions, especially repertories associated with poet-minstrels. Other recurring instruments include the *kemençe* fiddle of Black Sea regions, the reed flute *ney*, the double-reed *zurna*, the drum *davul*, and frame drums. Their meaning depends on context: the same instrument can appear at a wedding, in an ensemble, or on a concert stage.

Vocal styles range from unmetered long-air singing to strongly rhythmic dance songs. Texts address love, work, migration, landscape, humor, and religious devotion. Translation and transcription often lose dialect, ornamentation, and the social knowledge through which performers interpret lyrics.

## Regional dance forms

Many dances are performed in lines, circles, or linked formations. *Halay* is a broad term for line dances widespread in central, eastern, and southeastern areas, with local step patterns and tempos. Black Sea *horon* dances use compact, rapid movements, often accompanied by kemençe or bagpipes. Aegean *zeybek* forms tend toward measured, expansive gestures and may be performed solo or collectively.

The *horon*, *halay*, and *zeybek* labels each cover numerous variants. Costumes used by staged groups may draw on local garments but are often standardized for visibility and repeated performance. Social dancers adapt steps to age, ability, gender arrangements, and available space.

## Weddings and community performance

Weddings provide a major setting for music, dancing, and the public display of kin relations. Musicians coordinate changing groups of dancers, requests, and ritual moments such as the departure of a bride. In some localities, professional ensembles use amplified instruments; in others, relatives sing or play with limited accompaniment.

Musical participation also accompanies seasonal gatherings, military send-offs, and circumcision celebrations. These settings can bring together people who do not otherwise perform. They should be distinguished from the formally ordered *sema* ceremony, discussed in [Mevlevi music](/en/docs/turkiye/mevlevi-music).

## Transmission and representation

Apprenticeship, family memory, local teachers, recordings, and online videos all transmit repertoire. Folklore ensembles and municipal courses teach selected versions of dances, sometimes detaching them from the occasions in which they emerged. Ethnomusicological work therefore attends to both performance sound and the institutions that classify it.

## Variation

Whether a piece is performed for local participants, a paying audience, or a recording also changes the balance between continuity, display, and improvisation.

Regional repertoires overlap and change through migration, marriage, touring musicians, and recorded media. Turkish is not the only language of performance, and community histories affect which instruments, songs, and dances are preferred. A “national” folk presentation often contains many local traditions arranged under a shared frame. Performers may deliberately preserve an elder’s style, adapt it for a wedding band, or learn a standardized version in a dance course. Each route changes what counts as competent performance and who may claim knowledge of it.
