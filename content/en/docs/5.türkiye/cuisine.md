---
title: Cuisine in Türkiye
description: An overview of regional foodways, ingredients, meals, and culinary exchange in Türkiye.
---

Cuisine in Türkiye is organized through household meals, market provisioning, seasonal preservation, and forms of public eating. It encompasses practices associated with Anatolia, the Black Sea, the Aegean, Thrace, the Mediterranean, and southeastern regions, as well as urban cuisines shaped by migration and trade. Shared dishes acquire different ingredients, names, and social uses across these settings.

## Historical and geographic context

Anatolia’s position between the Black Sea, Aegean, Mediterranean, Caucasus, and Mesopotamian regions has encouraged movement of crops, techniques, and dining forms. Grain cultivation, sheep and goat herding, fishing, olive production, and horticulture provide distinct regional food bases. Ottoman-era urban kitchens, commercial routes, and later internal migration further circulated recipes without eliminating local distinctions.

## Staples, fats, and seasonality

Bread, bulgur, rice, legumes, yogurt, and vegetables form recurrent components of everyday meals. Wheat is prepared as loaves, flatbreads, noodles, pastries, and cracked grain; maize has particular importance in parts of the Black Sea region. Olive oil predominates in many western and southern vegetable dishes, while butter, clarified butter, and animal fat appear more often in colder inland or pastoral settings.

Seasonality governs both ingredients and labor. Summer vegetables are dried, pickled, bottled, or turned into pastes for winter use. Families make *salça*, a concentrated tomato or pepper paste, and preserve fruit as jams, syrups, or dried stores. These methods remain household practices but are also commercialized through markets and small producers.

## Soups, vegetables, and shared dishes

Soup commonly begins a meal and may be made with lentils, yogurt, grains, vegetables, or offal. Vegetable dishes include braises, stuffed leaves and vegetables, and preparations with pulses. The term *dolma* denotes things filled, while *sarma* generally refers to leaves rolled around a filling; actual usage varies by locality. In oil-based dishes, vegetables may be served at room temperature, whereas meat- or grain-based versions are commonly hot.

Meals often place several dishes in the center for collective service. Yogurt, salad, seasonal fruit, pickles, and bread balance a main preparation rather than operating as a fixed sequence. The familiar restaurant classification of “Turkish cuisine” therefore simplifies a broad collection of household and regional repertoires.

## Meat, fish, and dairy

Lamb, beef, poultry, and offal appear in domestic and commercial cooking, with frequency shaped by income, region, and occasion. Grilled or skewered meat is one visible public form, but stews, minced-meat fillings, soups, and dough dishes are equally important. In eastern and central areas, pastoral traditions have supported cheeses, yogurt, butter, and dried dairy products.

Coastal communities use locally available fish and shellfish, often prepared simply in season. Anchovy dishes are especially associated with the eastern Black Sea, while Aegean and Mediterranean cooking makes extensive use of herbs, greens, and seafood. Such associations should not be read as exclusive: fish, meat, and dairy products circulate through national supply systems.

## Sweets and beverages

Sweet foods range from fruit desserts and milk puddings to layered pastries and syrup-soaked doughs. *Baklava* has many regional forms distinguished by nut, fat, thickness, and method. *Aşure*, a cooked mixture of grains, legumes, dried fruit, and nuts, is prepared in some households and institutions during Muharram and distributed to neighbors.

Tea, called *çay*, is a routine accompaniment to conversation and food, while coffee is served in households and public venues. Ayran, a salted yogurt drink, and fruit sherbets also have enduring local and ceremonial uses. For sociability centered on drinks, see [Coffeehouses and tea gardens](/en/docs/turkiye/coffeehouse-tea-garden).

## Variation

Regional labels are useful starting points rather than closed culinary boundaries. Aegean olive-oil dishes, Black Sea maize and anchovy preparations, central Anatolian grain foods, southeastern spice and kebab traditions, and eastern dairy practices overlap through travel, commerce, and family movement. Household recipes also differ by religious community, rural or urban setting, and access to market ingredients.
